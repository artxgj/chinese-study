### 【中文詳解】煮豆燃萁

#### 一、成語基本資料

- 拼音：zhǔ dòu rán qí
- 字面意思：用豆莖（萁）當柴火來煮豆子。
- 比喻含義：比喻兄弟或親人之間因利害衝突而互相殘害、逼迫。
- 感情色彩：貶義。
- 常見用法：作謂語、定語、狀語；例如：「這兩兄弟為了遺產煮豆燃萁，實在令人痛心。」

---

#### 二、歷史淵源（《七步詩》的故事）

此成語出自三國時期曹魏。

- 人物：
  - 兄長 曹丕（後為魏文帝）
  - 弟弟 曹植（著名詩人）
- 背景：曹丕即位後，忌憚曹植的才華與聲望，擔心他威脅皇位。
- 關鍵事件：曹丕命曹植在走七步之內作詩一首，否則處死。曹植應聲吟出千古名作〈七步詩〉：

煮豆持作羹，漉菽以為汁。
萁在釜下燃，豆在釜中泣。
本是同根生，相煎何太急？

- 寓意：豆（曹植）與萁（曹丕）本出同根（父親曹操），如今卻被萁火煎熬，暗喻骨肉相殘。
- 結局：曹丕聽後慚愧，釋放了曹植。
- 史料來源：最早記載於南朝宋・劉義慶《世說新語・文學》，正史《三國志》未錄，故部分史家存疑，但故事本身已深植文化。

---

#### 三、現代用法與例句

- 用於指涉家庭內鬥、兄弟爭產、政黨派系自相殘殺等情境。
- 例句：
  - 「企業兩代接班人不應煮豆燃萁，而應合作共贏。」
  - 「這場內鬥完全是煮豆燃萁，讓外人看笑話。」

---

#### 四、近義與反義

- 近義成語：同室操戈、骨肉相殘、自相殘殺。
- 反義成語：兄友弟恭、手足情深、團結一心。

---

#### 五、總結

「煮豆燃萁」不僅濃縮了一段權力鬥爭的悲劇，更成為跨越時代的警世隱喻，提醒人們切勿因私利而傷害最親近的人。

### 【English Explanation】Zhǔ Dòu Rán Qí – Burning Beanstalks to Cook Beans

#### I. Basic Information

- Pinyin: zhǔ dòu rán qí
- Literal meaning: using beanstalks (qí) as fuel to boil beans (dòu).
- Figurative meaning: a metaphor for brothers or close relatives harming each other over power or profit – internecine strife.
- Connotation: pejorative.
- Usage: often used as a predicate, attributive, or adverbial. Example: “The two brothers engaged in zhǔ dòu rán qí over the inheritance, which saddened the whole family.”

---

#### II. Historical Origin – The Story of the “Seven-Step Poem”

The idiom originates from the Cao Wei dynasty during the Three Kingdoms period (c. 220–280 AD).

- Main figures:
  - Cao Pi (elder brother, later Emperor Wen of Wei)
  - Cao Zhi (younger brother, a renowned poet)
- Context: After ascending the throne, Cao Pi felt threatened by his brother’s literary talent and popularity, fearing he might contest the throne.
- The test: Cao Pi ordered Cao Zhi to compose a poem within the time it took to walk seven steps – on pain of death if he failed.
- Cao Zhi’s famous quatrain (in both classical Chinese and a poetic English rendering):

They boil the beans to make a soup,
Straining off the dregs for the juice.
The beanstalks blaze beneath the pot,
While in the pot the beans cry out:
“We sprouted from the selfsame root –
Why should you burn me so hot?”

(Translation adapted for rhythm and meaning.)

- Symbolism: The beans represent Cao Zhi; the beanstalks (qí) represent Cao Pi. Both come from the same root (their father, Cao Cao). The fire of the stalks tortures the beans, just as the elder brother torments the younger – a plea against fraternal cruelty.
- Outcome: Cao Pi, shamed by the poem, spared his brother.
- Historical note: The story first appears in A New Account of the Tales of the World (Shishuo Xinyu) by Liu Yiqing (Southern Dynasties). It is not recorded in the official Records of the Three Kingdoms, so some historians question its authenticity – yet its cultural impact is undeniable.

---

#### III. Modern Usage and Examples

Used to describe infighting among siblings, family feuds over estates, or factional conflicts within organisations.

- Examples:
  - “The two successors of the family business should avoid burning beanstalks to cook beans and instead cooperate for mutual benefit.”
  - “Their internal power struggle is a classic case of fratricidal strife – it only makes them look foolish to outsiders.”

---

#### IV. Synonyms and Antonyms

- Synonyms: tóng shì cāo gē (same room wielding spears – internal conflict), gǔ ròu xiāng cán (flesh and blood destroying each other), zì xiāng cán shā (mutual annihilation).
- Antonyms: xiōng yǒu dì gōng (elder brothers kind, younger brothers respectful), shǒu zú qíng shēn (deep affection among siblings), tuán jié yī xīn (united as one).

---

#### V. Summary

Zhǔ dòu rán qí encapsulates a tragic historical episode of ambition and jealousy. More importantly, it has endured as a universal warning: never sacrifice those closest to you for the sake of selfish gain.
