# Part 2: 中英雙語回答 (Bilingual Response)

## 中文部分

### 一、总论：雅言与方言的双层语言体系

春秋时期（公元前770年—公元前476年）的语言状况，可以概括为 **“雅言”与“方言”并存** 的双层体系。

**雅言** 是周王室推行的标准语，以中原一带（尤其是东周都城洛邑）的方言为基础。《论语·述而》记载：“子所雅言，《诗》《书》执礼，皆雅言也”——孔子在诵读经典和举行正式仪式时都使用雅言。雅言主要用于外交会盟、贵族交际、教育典籍等正式场合。

**方言** 则是各诸侯国民众日常使用的本土语言。许慎在《说文解字·序》中称各诸侯国“言语异声”，《礼记·王制》也记载“五方之民，言语不通，嗜欲不同”。方言之间的差异有大有小：秦、晋之间差别较小，而齐、楚之间以及秦、魏之间差异很大，甚至“相互之间很难听懂对方的话”。

春秋文字的发展大致经历了三个阶段：**春秋初期**，各国大体沿袭西周晚期的文字作风，差异不显著；**春秋中期以后**，随着周王室权威衰落，各区域逐渐形成以大国为核心的政治文化中心，文字形体开始呈现区域性特色；**春秋晚期至战国**，文字地域性差异进一步扩大。春秋文字大体上呈现 **南方诸国、山东齐鲁诸国、秦与中原诸国** 三大区域格局。在古文字学界，一般将春秋文字划分为 **秦系、楚系、晋系、齐系** 四大系统，有时也加入**燕系**形成“五系说”。

### 二、按地理区域分组的方言与文字

#### （一）中原地区（周王室及姬姓诸侯国——周、郑、卫、宋等）

**方言特点**：中原地区是雅言的核心区。周王室所在的王畿地区（洛邑一带）方言构成了雅言的基础。这一区域的方言与雅言最为接近，差异最小。

**文字特点**：中原诸侯国较多地保留了西周晚期的文字风格，变化较少。鲁国虽与齐国毗邻，但鲁国金文“罕见齐金文那种瘦长、工整而多垂笔的字体，多数器铭之书体仍较多地保留西周晚期金文的风格，稳重而敦厚，这也许与鲁国保存周礼最多有关”。

**代表性国家**：周、鲁、郑、卫、宋、许、蔡等。

#### （二）东方地区（齐系——齐、鲁、邾、滕、莒等）

**方言特点**：齐语是一个大的方言地域。齐语与吴语的差异很大，相互之间很难听懂。即使到了战国时期，楚国人要学说齐语，也需要“引而置之庄岳之间数年”才能掌握。

**文字特点**：齐系金文指铸刻在以齐国为中心，包括鲁、邾、郳、任、莒、杞、滕、薛等诸侯国青铜器上的文字。春秋早期齐系文字的代表如《齐萦姬盘》《鲁伯愈鬲》等，“结体疏朗平整，用笔细劲圆润，承接了西周晚期的金文风格，端庄中不乏灵动之姿”。春秋中期以后，齐系文字“结体逐渐由平整变为颀长”，出现了“狭长宛曲”的美术化字体。

**代表性国家**：齐、鲁、邾、滕、莒、杞、薛等。

#### （三）南方地区（楚系——楚、吴、越、曾、徐等）

**方言特点**：楚语与中原雅言差异较大。《左传》中有“楚言而出”的记载。越人的语言更为特殊，《越人歌》显示越语与今天的壮语、泰语近似，可能属于壮傣语支或南亚语系。吴国口语和越国类似，但吴国的外交家如延陵季子必须掌握雅言才能游走于中原各国。南方诸国普遍存在 **“双言现象”** ——雅言用于对外交流等正式场合（高层次变体），土话用于日常内部交流（低层次变体）。有一个有趣的案例：公元前666年，楚国司马子元在伐郑时，特意用楚语下令撤退，以避免被郑军识破军令。

**文字特点**：楚系文字在春秋中期开始形成自己的风格。楚系文字最具特色的发展是**鸟虫书**（又称鸟篆、虫书），这是一种极具装饰性的美术字体，流行于春秋晚期至战国早期，盛行于楚、吴、越、蔡、徐、宋等南方诸国。鸟虫书“利用线条的夸张摆动来进行装饰”，有的还在字中加入鸟（凤）的形状，“常以错金形式出现，高贵而华丽，富有装饰效果”。代表器物有楚王子午鼎铭、楚王孙渔戈等。

**代表性国家**：楚、吴、越、曾、徐、蔡等。

#### （四）西方地区（秦系——秦国）

**方言特点**：秦国的方言属于西方方言区。由于秦国是相对晚封的诸侯国，其对雅言的掌握“明显落后于早期封国”。但秦、晋之间方言差别较小。

**文字特点**：秦国“基本上继承了周人在宗周故地的统治范围”，在文字上“继承了西周晚期文字形体的发展路线，字形更加规整，线条更加匀称”。甘肃礼县大堡子山秦公墓地出土的青铜器铭文，是目前发现的最早秦国文字。春秋早期的秦文字“笔法初具篆意，但字体结构较为松散”；到了春秋晚期，以秦公簋铭文为代表，“字体更为规整”，“汉字方块化的趋势更加明确”。春秋时期的秦石刻文字（如石鼓文）大多继承殷商和西周以来的写法，“使文字具有一种古朴的风格”，而同时期的他国文字大多已出现简化字形。

**代表性国家**：秦。

#### （五）北方与中原过渡区（晋系——晋及三晋）

**方言特点**：晋语属于北方方言区，与秦语差别较小。晋国作为姬姓大国，地处中原与北方过渡带，其上层对雅言掌握程度较高。

**文字特点**：晋系文字最著名的代表是 **《侯马盟书》** ——1965年在山西侯马晋国遗址出土，是春秋晚期晋国六卿之一赵鞅在晋国都城新田主盟宣誓的辞文，用毛笔书写在玉石片上，有朱书和墨书。这些文字“落笔重而起笔轻，侧锋起笔，出锋收笔，结体生动自然，与同时期的金文风格不同”，具有“强烈的连笔意识和笔画的相互映带”。晋系文字在金文方面，“器铭布局较散，横不成排，字大小亦不同，但笔画细劲而多方折”。

**代表性国家**：晋及后来的赵、魏、韩。

#### （六）北方地区（燕系——燕国）

**方言特点**：燕国偏居北方，“与中原殊少往还”，方言具有自身特色。

**文字特点**：燕系文字“呈现出有自身特色的北方风格”。燕国国力相对较弱，文字资料发现较少。

**代表性国家**：燕。

### 三、各国如何沟通：口语与书面

**口语交流方面**：春秋时期各国之间的口语沟通主要依靠 **雅言**。各国诸侯会盟时“不需要翻译就可以实现沟通和交流”。《左传·襄公十四年》记载戎人代表驹支能赋《青蝇》参与会盟，反证了诸侯国之间可用同一种语言沟通。外交使节、游说之士、贵族学者都必须掌握雅言才能跨国务政、游学和交流。对于不能掌握雅言的普通民众，跨区域交流则需要翻译——比如楚国的鄂君子皙在听到越人船夫唱歌后完全听不懂，经人翻译才明白歌词含义。

**书面交流方面**：春秋时期各国虽然文字形体开始出现地域差异，但**书面交流的障碍远小于口语**。这是因为：

第一，各国文字均源于西周金文，基本字形结构相通。春秋初期各国文字“跟周晚期的金文没有什么显著的差别”。

第二，重要的外交文书、盟誓、典籍等都用雅言书写，采用相对规范的文字体系。侯马盟书虽是晋国之物，但其“字体用金文”，采用的是官方标准的文字系统。

第三，“赋诗言志”是春秋时期重要的外交文化活动，各国贵族在外交场合引用《诗经》中的诗句来表达政治立场和情感，这要求双方对经典文本有共同的理解。

### 四、存世文物与文字遗存

春秋时期留下了丰富的文字文物，主要包括以下几类：

**（一）青铜器铭文**

这是春秋文字最主要的载体。重要发现包括：

- **秦公簋**：1919年甘肃天水出土，春秋中期偏早，器盖连铭合计105字。
- **秦公镈、秦公编钟**：陕西宝鸡太公庙出土，同铭长达135字。
- **秦公大墓石磬铭文**：陕西凤翔南指挥秦公一号大墓出土，多枚。
- **齐萦姬盘、鲁伯愈鬲**：春秋早期齐系文字的代表。
- **王子午鼎**：春秋晚期楚器，鸟虫书代表。
- **楚王孙渔戈**：春秋晚期，错金鸟篆铭文，鸟虫书早期形态。
- **叔夷钟镈**：春秋时期齐器，铭文中有“禹”“夏”等重要历史记载。
- **曾公求、曾侯宝、曾侯得墓青铜器**：湖北随州枣树林春秋曾国墓地出土，铭文近6000字，发现了“禹”、“夏”铭文。

**（二）盟书**

- **侯马盟书**：1965年山西侯马出土，春秋晚期晋国，玉石片上朱书、墨书盟誓辞文，共数千件。

**（三）石刻文字**

- **石鼓文**：共十件，春秋末期秦国刻石，是现存最早的刻石文字之一。笔画匀整，结字舒朗，与秦公簋风格相近。

**（四）简牍**

春秋时期的简牍实物发现较少，但战国时期的楚简（如郭店楚简、望山楚简、信阳楚简）以及睡虎地秦简等，为研究从春秋到战国文字演变提供了珍贵材料。

这些文物表明，春秋文字正处于 **“上承文字形体较统一的西周金文，下启文字严重异形的战国文字”** 的过渡阶段。各国文字在继承西周传统的基础上，逐步发展出各自的地域特色。


## English Part

### I. Overview: The Dual-Language System of Yayan and Dialects

During the Spring and Autumn period (770–476 BCE), the linguistic landscape of China can be summarized as a **dual system of “Yayan” (雅言) and regional dialects** coexisting side by side.

**Yayan** was the standard language promoted by the Zhou royal court, based on the dialects of the Central Plains region, especially the area around the Eastern Zhou capital Luoyi. The *Analects* records: “When Confucius used Yayan, it was in reciting the *Book of Odes*, the *Book of Documents*, and performing the rites — all in Yayan”. Yayan was primarily used in diplomatic meetings, aristocratic communication, education, and classical texts.

**Dialects** were the everyday languages spoken by the common people of various states. Xu Shen in his *Shuowen Jiezi* noted that the states had “different speech sounds”, and the *Book of Rites* recorded that “the people of the five regions had mutually unintelligible languages and different desires”. The degree of difference varied: Qin and Jin dialects were relatively close, while Qi and Chu, or Qin and Wei, were so different that “they could hardly understand each other”.

The development of writing in the Spring and Autumn period can be divided into three stages: **Early Spring and Autumn** — most states followed the late Western Zhou writing style with little noticeable difference; **Mid-to-late Spring and Autumn** — as royal authority declined, regional political and cultural centers formed around major states, and scripts began to develop regional characteristics; **Late Spring and Autumn to Warring States** — regional differences in scripts further expanded. Spring and Autumn scripts generally show a **three-region pattern: the southern states, the Qi-Lu region in Shandong, and the Qin-Central Plains region**. In paleographic scholarship, Spring and Autumn scripts are typically divided into four major systems: **Qin, Chu, Jin, and Qi**, sometimes with the **Yan** system added to form a “five-system theory”.

### II. Dialects and Scripts Grouped by Geographic Region

#### (A) Central Plains Region (Royal Domain and Ji-surname States — Zhou, Zheng, Wei, Song, etc.)

**Dialect characteristics**: The Central Plains was the core area of Yayan. The dialect of the royal domain around Luoyi formed the foundation of Yayan. Dialects in this region were closest to Yayan.

**Script characteristics**: Central Plains states largely preserved late Western Zhou writing styles with fewer changes. Although Lu was adjacent to Qi, Lu bronze inscriptions “rarely show the elongated, neat, and vertical-stroke-heavy style of Qi inscriptions; most Lu inscriptions retain the steady and dignified late Western Zhou style, perhaps because Lu preserved Zhou rituals most thoroughly”.

**Representative states**: Zhou, Lu, Zheng, Wei, Song, Xu, Cai, etc.

#### (B) Eastern Region (Qi System — Qi, Lu, Zhu, Teng, Ju, etc.)

**Dialect characteristics**: Qi dialect formed a major dialect region. Qi and Wu dialects differed greatly and were mutually unintelligible. Even in the Warring States period, for a person from Chu to learn Qi dialect, it required “placing them in the Zhuang-Yue area for several years” to master it.

**Script characteristics**: Qi-system bronze inscriptions refer to texts cast on bronzes centered around Qi, including Lu, Zhu, Ni, Ren, Ju, Qi, Teng, Xue, and other states. Early Spring and Autumn representative works like the *Qi Yingji Pan* and *Lu Boyu Li* feature “spacious and neat structures with fine, rounded strokes, inheriting the late Western Zhou style with a lively grace”. From the mid-Spring and Autumn period onward, Qi script “gradually changed from neat to elongated”, and “narrow and curving” decorative scripts appeared.

**Representative states**: Qi, Lu, Zhu, Teng, Ju, Qi, Xue, etc.

#### (C) Southern Region (Chu System — Chu, Wu, Yue, Zeng, Xu, etc.)

**Dialect characteristics**: Chu dialect differed significantly from Central Plains Yayan. The *Zuo Commentary* records instances of “speaking Chu language”. The Yue language was even more distinctive — the *Yue Song* suggests it was related to modern Zhuang or Thai, possibly belonging to the Zhuang-Tai branch or Austroasiatic language family. Wu and Yue spoken languages were similar, but Wu diplomats like Yanling Jizi had to master Yayan to travel among Central Plains states. Southern states generally exhibited **“diglossia”** — Yayan was the high variety used in formal external communication, while local dialects were the low variety for daily internal use. An interesting case: in 666 BCE, when Chu general Zi Yuan was campaigning against Zheng, he deliberately gave orders in the Chu dialect to prevent the Zheng army from understanding his military commands.

**Script characteristics**: Chu script began developing its own style in the mid-Spring and Autumn period. The most distinctive development was **bird-and-worm script** (also called bird-seal or worm script) — a highly decorative ornamental script popular from late Spring and Autumn to early Warring States, flourishing in southern states including Chu, Wu, Yue, Cai, Xu, and Song. Bird-and-worm script “uses exaggerated undulating lines for decoration,” sometimes incorporating bird (phoenix) shapes into characters, and “often appears in gold inlay, magnificent and splendid with rich decorative effect”. Representative artifacts include the *Prince Wu Ding* vessel inscription and the *King of Chu’s Grandson Yu* dagger inscription.

**Representative states**: Chu, Wu, Yue, Zeng, Xu, Cai, etc.

#### (D) Western Region (Qin System — Qin)

**Dialect characteristics**: Qin belonged to the western dialect region. As a relatively late enfeoffed state, Qin’s mastery of Yayan “clearly lagged behind the earlier enfeoffed states”. However, Qin and Jin dialects were relatively close.

**Script characteristics**: Qin “essentially inherited the Zhou royal domain in the former Zongzhou region” and “inherited the developmental trajectory of late Western Zhou script, with more regular forms and more balanced strokes”. Bronze inscriptions from the Qin公 cemetery at Dabaozi Mountain in Li County, Gansu, are the earliest Qin script discovered to date. Early Qin script “shows the rudiments of seal-script style but with relatively loose structures”; by the late Spring and Autumn period, as seen in the *Qin Gong Gui* inscription, “the script became much more regular,” and “the trend toward square Han-character forms became more pronounced”. Spring and Autumn period Qin stone inscriptions (like the *Stone Drum Inscriptions*) largely inherited the writing styles of the Shang and Western Zhou, “giving the script an antique and simple quality,” whereas contemporary scripts in other states had mostly developed simplified forms.

**Representative states**: Qin.

#### (E) Northern and Central Plains Transition Zone (Jin System — Jin and the Three Jins)

**Dialect characteristics**: Jin belonged to the northern dialect region, with relatively small differences from Qin dialect. As a major Ji-surname state located in the transition zone between the Central Plains and the north, Jin’s upper class had a high command of Yayan.

**Script characteristics**: The most famous representative of Jin-system script is the **Houma Covenant Texts** — discovered in 1965 at the Jin state site in Houma, Shanxi. These are covenant oath texts from the late Spring and Autumn period, sworn by Zhao Yang, one of the six ministers of Jin, at the Jin capital Xintian. They were written with brush on jade and stone tablets, in both red and black ink. These characters “have heavy beginnings and light endings, with slanting brush entries and tapered exits, vivid and natural structures, quite different from contemporary bronze inscription styles,” showing “strong cursive connections and mutual呼应 between strokes”. In terms of bronze inscriptions, Jin script features “scattered layouts without horizontal alignment, varying character sizes, but thin and strong strokes with many angular turns”.

**Representative states**: Jin, and later Zhao, Wei, Han.

#### (F) Northern Region (Yan System — Yan)

**Dialect characteristics**: Yan was located in the far north, “with little contact with the Central Plains”, and its dialect had distinctive local features.

**Script characteristics**: Yan script “displayed a distinctive northern style”. Yan was relatively weak, and fewer script artifacts have been discovered.

**Representative states**: Yan.

### III. How States Communicated: Spoken and Written Forms

**Spoken communication**: Inter-state oral communication in the Spring and Autumn period relied primarily on **Yayan**. When feudal lords convened for alliances, “they could communicate and exchange views without interpreters”. The *Zuo Commentary* records that Ju Zhi, a representative of the Rong people, could recite the *Ode to the Green Fly* at an alliance meeting — proving that the states could use a common language. Diplomats, political advisors, and aristocratic scholars all had to master Yayan to conduct cross-state affairs, travel, and study. For common people who could not speak Yayan, cross-regional communication required interpreters — for example, when Ejun Zixi of Chu heard a Yue boatman singing, he could not understand it at all and needed a translation to grasp the meaning.

**Written communication**: Although regional script variations began to emerge during this period, **written communication faced far fewer obstacles than spoken communication**. This was due to several reasons:

First, all state scripts originated from Western Zhou bronze script, sharing basic structural similarities. In the early Spring and Autumn period, scripts “showed no significant difference from late Western Zhou bronze inscriptions”.

Second, important diplomatic documents, covenants, and classical texts were all written in Yayan, using a relatively standardized script system. The Houma Covenant Texts, though from Jin, “used bronze-script style characters” — the official standard script system.

Third, **“reciting poetry to express intentions”** (赋诗言志) was a major diplomatic cultural activity during the Spring and Autumn period. Aristocrats from different states would quote verses from the *Book of Odes* to express political positions and emotions in diplomatic settings, which required both parties to share a common understanding of the classical texts.

### IV. Surviving Artifacts and Script Remains

The Spring and Autumn period has left rich written artifacts,主要包括以下几类:

**(A) Bronze Inscriptions**

This is the primary carrier of Spring and Autumn script. Important discoveries include:

- **Qin Gong Gui** (秦公簋): Unearthed in Tianshui, Gansu in 1919, mid-Spring and Autumn period, with 105 characters on the vessel and cover combined.
- **Qin Gong Bo and Qin Gong Bells** (秦公镈、秦公编钟): Unearthed at Taigongmiao, Baoji, Shaanxi, with a long inscription of 135 characters.
- **Qin Gong Grand Tomb Stone Chime Inscriptions**: Unearthed from the Qin公 No.1 Grand Tomb at Nan Zhihui, Fengxiang, Shaanxi, multiple pieces.
- **Qi Yingji Pan and Lu Boyu Li**: Representative early Qi-system script artifacts.
- **Prince Wu Ding Vessel** (王子午鼎): Late Spring and Autumn Chu vessel, representative of bird-and-worm script.
- **King of Chu’s Grandson Yu Dagger** (楚王孙渔戈): Late Spring and Autumn, with gold-inlaid bird-seal script inscription, an early form of bird-and-worm script.
- **Shu Yi Bells** (叔夷钟镈): Spring and Autumn Qi vessel, with inscriptions containing important historical records including “Yu” and “Xia”.
- **Bronzes from the Zeng Gong Qiu, Zeng Hou Bao, and Zeng Hou De Tombs**: Excavated from the Zaoshulin cemetery in Suizhou, Hubei, with nearly 6,000 characters of inscription, including references to “Yu” and “Xia”.

**(B) Covenant Texts**

- **Houma Covenant Texts** (侯马盟书): Unearthed in Houma, Shanxi in 1965, late Spring and Autumn Jin state, with thousands of pieces of red and black ink oath texts on jade and stone tablets.

**(C) Stone Inscriptions**

- **Stone Drum Inscriptions** (石鼓文): Ten stone drums, late Spring and Autumn Qin stone carvings, among the earliest existing stone inscriptions. The strokes are even, the structures spacious, and the style similar to the Qin Gong Gui.

**(D) Bamboo and Wooden Slips**

Few bamboo slips from the Spring and Autumn period itself have been discovered, but Warring States period Chu slips (such as the Guodian Chu Slips, Wangshan Chu Slips, and Xinyang Chu Slips) and the Shuihudi Qin Slips provide valuable materials for studying the evolution of script from Spring and Autumn to Warring States.

These artifacts demonstrate that Spring and Autumn script was in a transitional phase — **“succeeding the relatively unified script of Western Zhou bronze inscriptions and preceding the highly divergent scripts of the Warring States”**. While inheriting Western Zhou traditions, the scripts of various states gradually developed their own regional characteristics.
