**三、詩意詳細解析**

**（一）寫作背景**

〈蜀道難〉是李白以樂府舊題創作的浪漫主義長詩，作於唐玄宗天寶初年（約742-744年），時李白初入長安，為送別友人入蜀而作 [1]。蜀道指由陝南穿越秦嶺、大巴山通往四川（古蜀地）的險峻道路。詩中極力描寫蜀道的艱險，並暗含對當時社會政治形勢的憂慮，被譽為「奇之又奇」之作 [2]。

**（二）字詞釋義**

- **噫吁嚱**：感嘆詞，表示驚嘆。
- **蜀道**：通往蜀地的道路，主要指秦嶺、大巴山中的棧道。
- **蠶叢、魚鳧**：傳說中古蜀國的兩位君主。
- **茫然**：渺茫難知。
- **爾來**：從那時以來。
- **秦塞**：秦地（今陝西）的關塞。
- **太白**：太白山，秦嶺主峰之一。
- **鳥道**：只有鳥才能飛過的道路，極言其險。
- **峨眉**：峨眉山，在四川。
- **地崩山摧**：傳說中秦惠王嫁五女於蜀，蜀王派五丁力士迎之，回至梓潼，見大蛇入山，五丁曳之，山崩地裂，五女與五丁皆死。
- **天梯石棧**：高聳入雲的梯子和架在石壁上的棧道。
- **六龍回日**：傳說太陽神駕六龍車，此處指山峰高聳，擋住太陽車，迫使六龍回轉。
- **高標**：最高處。
- **衝波**：激流。
- **逆折**：迴旋倒流。
- **黃鶴**：天鵝，善飛。
- **猿猱**：猿猴。
- **青泥**：青泥嶺，在今甘肅徽縣，為入蜀要道。
- **盤盤**：盤旋曲折貌。
- **捫參歷井**：參、井皆星宿名，言山高可觸及星辰。
- **脅息**：屏住呼吸。
- **撫膺**：拍胸。
- **坐**：徒然。
- **畏途**：可怕的路途。
- **巉岩**：險峻的山岩。
- **子規**：杜鵑鳥，啼聲哀切。
- **凋朱顏**：使紅顏衰老，比喻使人憂愁。
- **喧豗**：轟鳴聲。
- **砯崖**：水衝擊山崖。
- **萬壑雷**：千山萬谷中雷聲震盪。
- **劍閣**：劍門關，在今四川劍閣，為蜀地門戶。
- **崢嶸、崔嵬**：高峻貌。
- **一夫當關，萬夫莫開**：形容劍閣之險，一人把守，萬人難攻。
- **所守或匪親**：守關者若非親信。
- **狼與豺**：比喻叛亂者。
- **錦城**：成都。
- **咨嗟**：嘆息。

**（三）逐句解析**

本詩可分為三大段：開篇至「然後天梯石棧相鉤連」寫蜀道之由來與歷史；第二段從「上有六龍回日之高標」至「嗟爾遠道之人胡為乎來哉」寫蜀道之險峻與行旅之艱；第三段從「劍閣崢嶸而崔嵬」至結尾，寫蜀地之險與對友人的勸誡。

- **開篇「噫吁嚱，危乎高哉！蜀道之難，難於上青天！」**  
  以強烈的感嘆開場，奠定全詩雄偉驚險的基調。反復詠嘆「蜀道之難，難於上青天」，為全詩主旋律 [1]。

- **「蠶叢及魚鳧，開國何茫然！」**  
  追溯古蜀國歷史，開國之君年代久遠，渺茫難知，增添神話色彩。

- **「爾來四萬八千歲，不與秦塞通人煙。」**  
  極言時間之久，蜀地與中原隔絕，突出其封閉與神秘。

- **「西當太白有鳥道，可以橫絕峨眉巔。」**  
  寫太白山至峨眉山之間，僅有鳥道可通，突出險峻。

- **「地崩山摧壯士死，然後天梯石棧相鉤連。」**  
  引用五丁開山的神話，壯士之死換來棧道之通，暗示開闢之路的代價。

- **「上有六龍回日之高標，下有衝波逆折之回川。」**  
  上極高，下極深，對比強烈，極寫山川之險。

- **「黃鶴之飛尚不得過，猿猱欲度愁攀援。」**  
  善飛的黃鶴不能過，善攀的猿猱也發愁，側面烘托蜀道之難。

- **「青泥何盤盤，百步九折縈岩巒。」**  
  具體描寫青泥嶺的曲折，百步之內九道彎，纏繞山巒。

- **「捫參歷井仰脅息，以手撫膺坐長嘆。」**  
  山高可觸星，行人緊張屏息，手拍胸口長嘆，生動刻畫行旅者的恐懼。

- **「問君西遊何時還？畏途巉岩不可攀。」**  
  詩人轉而詢問友人何時歸來，告誡路途可怕，不可輕易攀登。

- **「但見悲鳥號古木，雄飛雌從繞林間。」**  
  以悲鳥哀號渲染淒涼氛圍，增添旅途之孤寂。

- **「又聞子規啼夜月，愁空山。」**  
  子規（杜鵑）啼聲淒厲，夜月下更顯空山悲涼。

- **「蜀道之難，難於上青天，使人聽此凋朱顏！」**  
  再次詠嘆，聽聞此景令人失色，感嘆之深。

- **「連峰去天不盈尺，枯松倒掛倚絕壁。」**  
  山峰離天不過咫尺，枯松倒掛懸崖，極寫高與險。

- **「飛湍瀑流爭喧豗，砯崖轉石萬壑雷。」**  
  急流瀑布轟鳴，水擊山崖，巨石滾落，聲如萬雷，渲染驚心動魄之勢。

- **「其險也如此，嗟爾遠道之人胡為乎來哉！」**  
  詩人感嘆：如此險惡，你為何還要遠道而來？表達對友人的關切。

- **「劍閣崢嶸而崔嵬，一夫當關，萬夫莫開。」**  
  轉入劍閣之險，一人守關，萬人難攻，形勢嚴峻。

- **「所守或匪親，化為狼與豺。」**  
  若守將非親信，則可能叛變，化為豺狼，暗含政治隱憂。

- **「朝避猛虎，夕避長蛇；磨牙吮血，殺人如麻。」**  
  早晚躲避野獸，象徵戰亂環境之險惡。

- **「錦城雖雲樂，不如早還家。」**  
  錦官城雖說快樂，但不如早日回家，直抒勸歸之意。

- **「蜀道之難，難於上青天，側身西望長咨嗟！」**  
  末句再次詠嘆，詩人側身西望，長久嘆息，餘韻無窮。

**（四）整體意境與主題**

〈蜀道難〉以誇張、神話和想像，極力渲染蜀道之險峻，展現了李白浪漫主義的極致才華 [1]。其主題有多重解讀：

- **送別勸誡**：表面上是為友人入蜀而作，反覆勸其早歸。
- **政治隱喻**：詩中「所守或匪親，化為狼與豺」、「朝避猛虎，夕避長蛇」等句，可能暗諷當時蜀地節度使擁兵自重、叛亂頻仍的政局，或對朝廷用人失當的憂慮 [2]。
- **人生感嘆**：蜀道之難，亦可象徵人生道路的艱險坎坷，體現詩人對世路的深刻體悟。

**（五）藝術特色**

- **反覆詠嘆**：「蜀道之難，難於上青天」出現三次，一唱三嘆，強化主題。
- **誇張與想像**：上抵日月星辰，下至深淵激流，神話與現實交融，氣勢磅礡。
- **句式參差**：長短句交錯，節奏變化多端，與蜀道之崎嶇相呼應。
- **渲染烘托**：通過鳥獸、聲音、神話等從側面烘托蜀道之險，增強感染力。

猿猱欲度愁攀援。  
yuán náo yù dù chóu pān yuán。  

青泥何盘盘，  
qīng ní hé pán pán，  

百步九折萦岩峦。  
bǎi bù jiǔ zhé yíng yán luán。  

扪参历井仰胁息，  
mén shēn lì jǐng yǎng xié xī，  

以手抚膺坐长叹。  
yǐ shǒu fǔ yīng zuò cháng tàn。  

问君西游何时还？  
wèn jūn xī yóu hé shí huán？  

畏途巉岩不可攀。  
wèi tú chán yán bù kě pān。  

但见悲鸟号古木，  
dàn jiàn bēi niǎo háo gǔ mù，  

雄飞雌从绕林间。  
xióng fēi cí cóng rào lín jiān。  

又闻子规啼夜月，  
yòu wén zǐ guī tí yè yuè，  

愁空山。  
chóu kōng shān。  

蜀道之难，  
shǔ dào zhī nán，  

难于上青天，  
nán yú shàng qīng tiān，  

使人听此凋朱颜！  
shǐ rén tīng cǐ diāo zhū yán！  

连峰去天不盈尺，  
lián fēng qù tiān bù yíng chǐ，  

枯松倒挂倚绝壁。  
kū sōng dào guà yǐ jué bì。  

飞湍瀑流争喧豗，  
fēi tuān pù liú zhēng xuān huī，  

砯崖转石万壑雷。  
pīng yá zhuǎn shí wàn hè léi。  

其险也如此，  
qí xiǎn yě rú cǐ，  

嗟尔远道之人胡为乎来哉！  
jiē ěr yuǎn dào zhī rén hú wéi hū lái zāi！  

剑阁峥嵘而崔嵬，  
jiàn gé zhēng róng ér cuī wéi，  

一夫当关，  
yī fū dāng guān，  

万夫莫开。  
wàn fū mò kāi。  

所守或匪亲，  
suǒ shǒu huò fěi qīn，  

化为狼与豺。  
huà wéi láng yǔ chái。  

朝避猛虎，  
zhāo bì měng hǔ，  

夕避长蛇；  
xī bì cháng shé；  

磨牙吮血，  
mó yá shǔn xuè，  

杀人如麻。  
shā rén rú má。  

锦城虽云乐，  
jǐn chéng suī yún lè，  

不如早还家。  
bù rú zǎo huán jiā。  

蜀道之难，  
shǔ dào zhī nán，  

难于上青天，  
nán yú shàng qīng tiān，  

侧身西望长咨嗟！  
cè shēn xī wàng cháng zī jiē！


**III. Detailed Analysis**

**(1) Historical Background**

"Shu Dao Nan" is a romantic long poem written by Li Bai in the early Tianbao era (c. 742–744) of Emperor Xuanzong, when he first arrived in Chang'an, to see off a friend on his journey to Shu (present-day Sichuan) [1]. The "Shu Road" refers to the perilous mountain paths through the Qinling and Daba ranges connecting Shaanxi to Sichuan. The poem vividly depicts the dangers of the road and contains implicit political concerns, earning it the praise of "strange beyond strangeness" [2].

**(2) Vocabulary Explanations**

- **噫吁嚱 (Yī Xū Xī)**: An interjection expressing surprise.
- **蜀道 (Shǔ Dào)**: The road to Shu, mainly referring to the plank roads in the Qinling and Daba mountains.
- **蠶叢、魚鳧 (Cán Cóng, Yú Fú)**: Legendary kings of ancient Shu.
- **茫然 (Máng Rán)**: Vague, uncertain.
- **爾來 (Ěr Lái)**: Since then.
- **秦塞 (Qín Sài)**: The passes of Qin (modern Shaanxi).
- **太白 (Tài Bái)**: Mount Taibai, one of the main peaks of the Qinling range.
- **鳥道 (Niǎo Dào)**: A path passable only by birds, indicating extreme danger.
- **峨眉 (É Méi)**: Mount Emei in Sichuan.
- **地崩山摧 (Dì Bēng Shān Cuī)**: Reference to the myth of the five "Strength" giants who died pulling a snake, causing a mountain collapse.
- **天梯石棧 (Tiān Tī Shí Zhàn)**: High ladders and plank roads built on cliffs.
- **六龍回日 (Liù Lóng Huí Rì)**: The sun chariot is said to be pulled by six dragons; here it refers to the mountain peaks blocking the sun.
- **高標 (Gāo Biāo)**: The highest point.
- **衝波 (Chōng Bō)**: Surging waves.
- **逆折 (Nì Zhé)**: Flowing backward in whirlpools.
- **黃鶴 (Huáng Hè)**: A swan or crane, known for its ability to fly high.
- **猿猱 (Yuán Náo)**: Monkeys.
- **青泥 (Qīng Ní)**: Qingni Ridge, a dangerous pass on the Shu Road.
- **盤盤 (Pán Pán)**: Winding and twisting.
- **捫參歷井 (Mén Shēn Lì Jǐng)**: Touching the constellations Shen (Orion) and Jing (Gemini), indicating extreme height.
- **脅息 (Xié Xī)**: Holding one's breath.
- **撫膺 (Fǔ Yīng)**: Patting one's chest.
- **坐 (Zuò)**: In vain.
- **畏途 (Wèi Tú)**: A frightening road.
- **巉岩 (Chán Yán)**: Steep, precipitous rocks.
- **子規 (Zǐ Guī)**: The cuckoo, whose cry is mournful.
- **凋朱顏 (Diāo Zhū Yán)**: To make the rosy complexion fade, metaphor for causing worry.
- **喧豗 (Xuān Huī)**: Roaring noise.
- **砯崖 (Pīng Yá)**: Water crashing against rocks.
- **萬壑雷 (Wàn Hè Léi)**: Thunderous sound echoing through the valleys.
- **劍閣 (Jiàn Gé)**: Jiange Pass, a strategic gateway to Shu.
- **崢嶸、崔嵬 (Zhēng Róng, Cuī Wéi)**: Tall and rugged.
- **一夫當關，萬夫莫開 (Yī Fū Dāng Guān, Wàn Fū Mò Kāi)**: One man defending the pass can keep out ten thousand.
- **所守或匪親 (Suǒ Shǒu Huò Fěi Qīn)**: If the defender is not a trusted relative.
- **狼與豺 (Láng Yǔ Chái)**: Wolves and jackals, metaphors for rebels.
- **錦城 (Jǐn Chéng)**: Chengdu, the capital of Shu.
- **咨嗟 (Zī Jiē)**: To sigh.

**(3) Line-by-Line Interpretation**

The poem can be divided into three main sections: from the beginning to "then the ladders and plank roads connect," describing the origins and legends of the Shu Road; from "above are the high peaks..." to "why have you come here," depicting the extreme danger of the road; and from "Jiange Pass is rugged..." to the end, focusing on the local perils and the warning to the friend.

- **Opening: "Alas! How perilous and high! / The Shu Road is hard, harder than climbing to the blue sky!"**  
  The poem opens with powerful exclamations, setting a tone of awe and fear. The refrain, "The Shu Road is hard, harder than climbing to the blue sky," recurs to emphasize the theme [1].

- **"The kings Can Cong and Yu Fu / Founded a state, how dim and lost!"**  
  Recalling the ancient Shu kingdom, whose origins are shrouded in myth, adding a sense of mystery.

- **"For forty-eight thousand years, / It did not communicate with the Qin passes."**  
  Hyperbole for the long isolation of Shu, stressing its remoteness.

- **"To the west, Mount Taibai has a bird's path, / That can cross over the top of Mount Emei."**  
  Describing the only route between Taibai and Emei, a treacherous bird path.

- **"Mountains crumbled, heroes died, / Then the sky-ladders and stone plank roads were joined."**  
  Using the myth of the five giants who died to open the road, implying the great cost of passage.

- **"Above, peaks that turn back the sun's six dragons; / Below, rushing waves that turn back in whirlpools."**  
  Extreme height and depth, creating a stark contrast of danger.

- **"Even the yellow crane cannot fly across; / Monkeys, wanting to climb, are vexed."**  
  Even the best fliers and climbers are daunted, emphasizing the impossibility of the road.

- **"The green mud slope twists and turns, / A hundred steps, nine bends, around the rocky crags."**  
  A concrete description of the tortuous path on Qingni Ridge.

- **"Gasping, I touch the stars and lean on the sky; / Patting my chest, I sit and sigh."**  
  The traveller's fear and exhaustion are vividly portrayed.

- **"When will you return from your western journey? / The road is fearful, the cliffs cannot be climbed."**  
  The poet questions his friend and warns him of the danger.

- **"Only sad birds cry in the ancient trees, / Males flying, females following, circling the woods."**  
  Mournful birds enhance the desolate atmosphere.

- **"Again I hear the cuckoo crying at the night moon, / Empty mountains filled with sorrow."**  
  The cuckoo's mournful call under the moon deepens the pathos.

- **"The Shu Road is hard, harder than climbing to the blue sky, / Hearing this makes your rosy face fade!"**  
  The refrain returns, now with a personal impact.

- **"Ridge after ridge, less than a foot from the sky; / Withered pines hang upside-down, leaning on the cliff."**  
  Exaggerating the nearness to heaven and the terrifying appearance of the pines.

- **"Torrents and waterfalls race with a deafening roar; / Rocks are dashed and rolled, thunder in ten thousand valleys."**  
  The soundscape is overwhelming and terrifying.

- **"It is so dangerous! / Why have you come here, you distant wanderer?"**  
  The poet directly questions his friend's decision to travel here.

- **"Jiange Pass towers rugged and high; / One man can guard the pass, ten thousand cannot break through."**  
  The strategic and natural fortification of Jiange.

- **"But if the guard is not your own, / He will turn into wolf and jackal."**  
  A warning about the possibility of rebellion and betrayal.

- **"In the morning, avoid the fierce tigers; / In the evening, shun the long snakes; / Sharpening teeth and sucking blood, / Killing people like hemp."**  
  The constant danger from both wild beasts and violent men.

- **"Though they say Jin City (Chengdu) is joyful, / It is better to return home early."**  
  A direct urging to his friend to come back.

- **"The Shu Road is hard, harder than climbing to the blue sky, / I turn my head westward and sigh long!"**  
  The final refrain, leaving the poet in a posture of longing and worry.

**(4) Overall Mood and Themes**

"Shu Dao Nan" uses hyperbole, myth, and imagination to magnify the perils of the Shu Road, showcasing Li Bai's romantic genius [1]. Multiple interpretations of its themes exist:

- **Farewell and Warning**: On the surface, it is a farewell poem warning his friend to return early.
- **Political Allegory**: Phrases like "if the guard is not your own, he will turn into wolf and jackal" and "avoid the fierce tigers... long snakes" may allude to local military governors' threats of rebellion or the court's misappointments [2].
- **Philosophical Reflection**: The difficulty of the road can also symbolize the hardships of life itself.

**(5) Artistic Features**

- **Recurring Refrain**: "The Shu Road is hard, harder than climbing to the blue sky" appears three times, creating a powerful rhythm.
- **Exaggeration and Imagination**: From the sun-dragon to the star-touching peaks, the poem blends myth and reality with immense energy.
- **Variable Syntax**: Long and short lines alternate, mirroring the tortuous paths.
- **Indirect Description**: Using birds, animals, sounds, and legends to intensify the impact of the dangers.
