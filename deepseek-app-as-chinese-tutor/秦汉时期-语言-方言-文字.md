# Part 2: 中英雙語回答 (Bilingual Response)

## 中文部分

### 一、秦朝：以“书同文”奠定统一基石

秦统一六国后，面对战国时期“言语异声，文字异形”的混乱局面[reference:74]，秦始皇采纳丞相李斯的建议，推行了影响深远的 **“书同文”** 政策[reference:75]。秦朝的文字统一工作并非始于统一天下之时，而是随着其武力及政治势力的扩张而逐步实施的[reference:76][reference:77]。

**核心内容是“罢其不与秦文合者”**[reference:78][reference:79]——废除六国文字中与秦系写法不同的区域性异体字，并非强行创造一套全新文字[reference:80]。秦朝官方正式文书以**小篆**为正体，但实际运用中更广泛的是书写更为便利的**隶书**[reference:81][reference:82]。里耶秦简等出土材料证实，秦朝无论是民间还是官府文书，普遍使用的都是隶书而非小篆[reference:83]。

**方言方面**，秦朝虽统一了文字，但各地的方言依然保留[reference:84]。秦朝试图通过“书同文”从文字和用语上消除各方言人群的隔阂[reference:85]，并推行“语同音”政策，但其享祚不长，未能完全实现[reference:86]。

### 二、汉代方言的地域划分

西汉定都长安，关中为全国政治、经济和文化中心，**秦晋方言**成为当时的中心方言[reference:87]。西汉语言学家**扬雄**在其著作《輶轩使者绝代语释别国方言》（简称《方言》）中，系统记录了汉代方言的分布[reference:88][reference:89]。

综合《方言》、《史记·货殖列传》、《汉书·地理志》等文献，汉代汉语方言可划分为**十二个方言区**[reference:90][reference:91]：

1. **秦晋方言区**：秦、晋、梁益
2. **周韩郑方言区**：周、韩、郑
3. **赵魏方言区**：赵、魏
4. **卫宋方言区**：卫、宋
5. **齐鲁方言区**：齐、鲁
6. **东齐海岱方言区**：东齐、海岱
7. **燕代方言区**
8. **北燕朝鲜方言区**：北燕、朝鲜
9. **楚方言区**：楚郢、北楚、江淮
10. **南楚方言区**：江湘、沅澧、九嶷湘潭
11. **南越方言区**
12. **吴越方言区**：吴、越、瓯

此外，有学者进一步细化为**14种方言**甚至**16个方言区**[reference:92]。值得注意的是，某些方言中还夹杂着非汉语成分，如东齐青徐之夷、西秦之羌、南楚之蛮、北燕之东胡等[reference:93]。

两汉之交，秦晋方言已糅合为一，在全国占有最重要的地位。后代北方汉语正是以当时的秦晋与雒阳一带的方言为基础而逐渐定型的[reference:94]。

### 三、秦隶与汉隶的差异

秦朝和汉朝虽然都使用隶书，但两者存在显著差异：

**秦隶（古隶）**：处于过渡阶段，还保留了大量篆书的遗迹。字形尚未完全变为扁方，书写较为自由[reference:95]。睡虎地秦简的隶书虽然增加了一些篆书不具备的新笔法，但总体上仍具有过渡性，尚不成熟[reference:96]。

**汉隶（今隶/八分）**：在汉代发展成熟，笔画波磔分明，形态略扁[reference:97]。汉隶解散了篆体结构，形成了“蚕头雁尾”的典型笔画特征[reference:98]。

西汉早期的隶书（如银雀山汉简）仍属于“古隶”，篆书遗迹还比较多[reference:99]。从汉武帝中晚期以后，隶书逐渐趋于成熟[reference:100]。东汉中后期，随着隶书的定型化，开始被官方所重视[reference:101]。

### 四、汉代文字标准的建立——近三百年历程

汉代文字标准的建立并非一蹴而就，而是经历了**从西汉到东汉近三百年的漫长演变**[reference:102]：

1. **西汉初期（公元前202年—约公元前140年）**：沿袭秦制，使用“古隶”，篆隶并存，尚未形成统一标准[reference:103]。

2. **西汉中后期（约公元前140年—公元9年）**：隶书逐渐趋于成熟，但主要通行于民间和日常书写，尚未成为官方标准[reference:104]。

3. **东汉前期（公元25年—公元146年）**：隶书进一步发展，字形、笔势不断演变[reference:105]。东汉中期出现了庄重典雅的新隶体[reference:106]。

4. **东汉桓灵时期（公元146年—公元189年）**：石刻盛行，促使汉隶迅速臻于全面成熟[reference:107]。

5. **正式确立标准（公元175年）**：汉灵帝**熹平四年**，朝廷以新隶体将儒家经典刻立于**《熹平石经》**，置于洛阳太学，成为国家的**标准书体**。后人称之为 **“八分”** [reference:108][reference:109]。

同一时期，**许慎**编纂了**《说文解字》**（公元100—121年），以小篆为字头，系统分析汉字构形，为文字规范提供了理论依据[reference:110]。

**结论**：从秦朝推行“书同文”（公元前221年）到东汉《熹平石经》确立标准隶书（公元175年），中国文字经历了**近四个世纪**的演变。若从西汉建立算起，汉代文字标准的形成也历时**约375年**。这一漫长过程体现了文字演变的渐进性，而非一蹴而就的变革。


## English Part

### I. The Qin Dynasty: Laying the Foundation with "Script Unification"

After unifying China, Qin Shi Huang faced a situation where "speech differed in sound and writing differed in form" from the Warring States period[reference:111]. Following the advice of Chancellor Li Si, he implemented the far-reaching policy of **"Shu Tong Wen" (Unification of Script)** [reference:112]. This work did not begin only after unification but was implemented gradually alongside the expansion of Qin's military and political influence[reference:113][reference:114].

The core principle was **"abolishing what did not conform to Qin script"** [reference:115][reference:116]—eliminating regional variant characters from the eastern states that differed from Qin writing[reference:117]. The official script was **Small Seal Script**, but in practice, the more convenient **Clerical Script** was more widely used[reference:118][reference:119]. Excavated materials like the Liye Qin slips confirm that both official and civilian documents predominantly used clerical script, not seal script[reference:120].

Regarding **dialects**, although the Qin unified the script, regional dialects persisted[reference:121]. The Qin attempted to eliminate barriers between dialect groups through script unification[reference:122] and promoted "unified speech," but the dynasty was too short-lived to fully achieve this[reference:123].

### II. Regional Division of Han Dynasty Dialects

With Chang'an as the capital, the Guanzhong region became the political, economic, and cultural center. The **Qin-Jin dialect** became the central dialect of the time[reference:124]. The Western Han linguist **Yang Xiong** systematically recorded the distribution of Han dialects in his work *Fangyan* (Dialects)[reference:125][reference:126].

Based on *Fangyan*, *Records of the Grand Historian*, *Book of Han*, and other texts, Han dynasty dialects can be divided into **twelve major dialect regions**[reference:127][reference:128]:

1. **Qin-Jin dialect region**: Qin, Jin, Liang-Yi
2. **Zhou-Han-Zheng dialect region**: Zhou, Han, Zheng
3. **Zhao-Wei dialect region**: Zhao, Wei
4. **Wei-Song dialect region**: Wei, Song
5. **Qi-Lu dialect region**: Qi, Lu
6. **Eastern Qi-Hai-Dai dialect region**: Eastern Qi, Hai-Dai
7. **Yan-Dai dialect region**
8. **Northern Yan-Korea dialect region**: Northern Yan, Korea
9. **Chu dialect region**: Chu Ying, Northern Chu, Jiang-Huai
10. **Southern Chu dialect region**: Jiang-Xiang, Yuan-Li, Jiuyi-Xiangtan
11. **Southern Yue dialect region**
12. **Wu-Yue dialect region**: Wu, Yue, Ou

Some scholars have further refined this into **14 dialects** or even **16 dialect regions**[reference:129]. Notably, some dialects incorporated non-Chinese elements[reference:130].

By the end of the Western Han, Qin and Jin dialects had merged and occupied the most important position nationwide. Later northern Chinese was gradually shaped based on the Qin-Jin and Luoyang dialects[reference:131].

### III. Differences Between Qin Clerical Script and Han Clerical Script

Although both dynasties used clerical script, there were significant differences:

**Qin Clerical Script (Ancient Clerical Script)** : In a transitional phase, retaining many seal script features. Characters had not yet become fully flat and square, and writing was relatively free[reference:132]. While incorporating new brush techniques, it remained transitional and immature[reference:133].

**Han Clerical Script (Mature Clerical Script / Bafen)** : Matured during the Han dynasty, with distinct wavelike strokes (wave-tail) and a slightly flat shape[reference:134]. It dissolved the seal script structure, developing the characteristic "silkworm head and swallow tail" strokes[reference:135].

Early Western Han clerical script (like the Yinqueshan Han slips) still belonged to "ancient clerical script" with many seal script traces[reference:136]. From the mid-to-late Emperor Wu period onward, clerical script gradually matured[reference:137]. In the mid-to-late Eastern Han, as clerical script became standardized, it began to receive official attention[reference:138].

### IV. Establishment of the Han Dynasty Script Standard — A Nearly 300-Year Journey

The establishment of a script standard in the Han dynasty was not achieved overnight but underwent **nearly 300 years of evolution from the Western Han to the Eastern Han**[reference:139]:

1. **Early Western Han (202 BCE – c. 140 BCE)** : Continued Qin practices with "ancient clerical script." Seal and clerical scripts coexisted without a unified standard[reference:140].

2. **Mid-to-late Western Han (c. 140 BCE – 9 CE)** : Clerical script gradually matured but was mainly used among the people and in daily writing, not yet an official standard[reference:141].

3. **Early Eastern Han (25 CE – 146 CE)** : Clerical script further evolved with continuous changes in form and brushwork[reference:142]. A solemn and elegant new style emerged in the mid-Eastern Han[reference:143].

4. **Emperor Huan-Ling period (146 CE – 189 CE)** : The prevalence of stone inscriptions propelled clerical script to full maturity[reference:144].

5. **Official Standard Established (175 CE)** : In the **4th year of Xiping**, the court engraved the Confucian classics in the new clerical script on the **Xiping Stone Classics**, placed at the Imperial Academy in Luoyang, establishing it as the **national standard script**. Later generations called this **"Bafen"** (Eight-Part script)[reference:145][reference:146].

Around the same period, **Xu Shen** compiled the **Shuowen Jiezi** (c. 100–121 CE), which used Small Seal Script as headings and systematically analyzed character structures, providing a theoretical foundation for script standardization[reference:147].

**Conclusion**: From the Qin's "script unification" (221 BCE) to the Xiping Stone Classics establishing the standard clerical script (175 CE), Chinese writing underwent **nearly four centuries** of evolution. Calculated from the beginning of the Han dynasty, the formation of the Han script standard also took **approximately 375 years**. This long process demonstrates the gradual, evolutionary nature of script development rather than sudden, revolutionary change.
