## 2. Bilingual Response (Corrected with Numeric Citations)

### Chinese Section

**摘要**  
宋词是宋代文学的巅峰代表，是一种合乐歌唱的“长短句”诗歌 [1][3]。它的核心特点是**依词牌填词**——每个词牌都有固定的字数、句数、平仄和押韵规则 [2]。词按字数分为小令（58字内）、中调（59-90字）和长调（91字以上）[2][3]。本文将用苏轼《水调歌头》[5]、李清照《声声慢》[6][8]、辛弃疾《永遇乐》[7][9] 等名作逐条解释这些规则，最后附上著名词人及作品列表 [4]。

---

**详细说明**

#### 一、什么是宋词？

宋词是继唐诗之后的一种新体诗歌，又称“曲子词”、“长短句”、“诗馀”。它本是配合音乐歌唱的歌词，所以叫“填词”或“倚声”。宋词始于唐代，形成于五代，极盛于两宋。现存的《全宋词》收录了1300多位词人的约两万首作品 [1][3]。

宋词主要分两大流派 [4]：
- **婉约派**：风格柔美含蓄，多写男女之情和离愁别绪，代表词人有晏殊、柳永、李清照、周邦彦等。
- **豪放派**：风格雄浑豪迈，突破“词为艳科”的传统，以诗为词，表现家国情怀，代表词人有苏轼、辛弃疾、陆游等。

---

#### 二、词牌：词的“格式名称”

**词牌**是词调的名称，相当于今天的“歌谱名称”。它不是题目，只是标明这首词该按什么曲调来唱。比如《水调歌头》是词牌名，“明月几时有”才是这首词的题目（或叫词题）。

词牌总数超过一千个，每个词牌都有自己固定的格式（称“词谱”）。词人必须“依调填词”，严格遵守该词牌的字数、平仄、押韵等规定 [2]。

---

#### 三、宋词的三大形式规则（附名作例句）

##### 规则1：字数与句数——每首词的字数和句数都是固定的

每个词牌都规定了**多少字、多少句**，不能随意增减。按字数多少，词分为三类 [2][3]：
- **小令**：58字以内（如《如梦令》）
- **中调**：59–90字（如《蝶恋花》）
- **长调**：91字以上（如《水调歌头》《声声慢》《永遇乐》）

**举例——苏轼《水调歌头·明月几时有》** [5]

> 明月几时有？把酒问青天。不知天上宫阙，今夕是何年。我欲乘风归去，又恐琼楼玉宇，高处不胜寒。起舞弄清影，何似在人间！
>
> 转朱阁，低绮户，照无眠。不应有恨，何事长向别时圆？人有悲欢离合，月有阴晴圆缺，此事古难全。但愿人长久，千里共婵娟。

此词为**双调九十五字**，属长调。全词分上下两片（上片写问天，下片写怀人），上片九句，下片十句 [5]。

---

##### 规则2：平仄——每个字都有声调规定

“平仄”指汉字声调。古汉语分平声、上声、去声、入声（入声已融入现代普通话）。**平**是平声，**仄**是上、去、入三声。词的平仄规定比唐诗更严格——每个位置该用平声还是仄声都是固定的 [2]。

**举例——李清照《声声慢·寻寻觅觅》** [6][8]

> 寻寻觅觅，冷冷清清，凄凄惨惨戚戚。乍暖还寒时候，最难将息。三杯两盏淡酒，怎敌他、晚来风急！雁过也，正伤心，却是旧时相识。
>
> 满地黄花堆积，憔悴损，如今有谁堪摘？守着窗儿，独自怎生得黑！梧桐更兼细雨，到黄昏、点点滴滴。这次第，怎一个愁字了得！

这首词**九十七字，上下片各五仄韵**。全词押入声韵（“戚”“息”“急”“识”“积”“摘”“黑”“滴”“得”都是入声字），声调短促急迫，与词中凄凉的情感完美配合。开篇连用七组叠字（寻寻、觅觅、冷冷、清清、凄凄、惨惨、戚戚），平仄交错，制造出如泣如诉的音乐效果 [6][8]。

---

##### 规则3：押韵——可以押平声韵，也可以押仄声韵，还可以换韵

词不像唐诗那样只能押平声韵。词的押韵灵活得多：可以全押平韵、全押仄韵，也可以平仄换韵 [2]。

**举例——辛弃疾《永遇乐·京口北固亭怀古》** [7][9]

> 千古江山，英雄无觅，孙仲谋处。舞榭歌台，风流总被，雨打风吹去。斜阳草树，寻常巷陌，人道寄奴曾住。想当年，金戈铁马，气吞万里如虎。
>
> 元嘉草草，封狼居胥，赢得仓皇北顾。四十三年，望中犹记，烽火扬州路。可堪回首，佛狸祠下，一片神鸦社鼓。凭谁问，廉颇老矣，尚能饭否？

此词**双调一百零四字，上下片各四仄韵**。全词押仄声韵（“处”“去”“住”“虎”“顾”“路”“鼓”“否”），韵脚铿锵有力，与词中怀古的雄浑气势相得益彰 [7][9]。

---

#### 四、词的结构：单片与双片

大部分词分**上下两片**（也称上下阕）。上片一般写景或叙事，下片抒情或议论。不分片的叫“单调” [3]。

### English Section

**Abstract**  
Song Ci is the crowning poetic achievement of the Song Dynasty—a type of "long-and-short-line" poetry written to be sung to music [1][3]. Its core feature is **composing according to cipai** (tune patterns), each with fixed rules on character count, line count, tonal pattern (level/oblique tones), and rhyme scheme [2]. Ci are classified by length: *xiaoling* (≤58 characters), *zhongdiao* (59–90), and *changdiao* (≥91) [2][3]. This article explains each rule with famous examples from Su Shi [5], Li Qingzhao [6][8], and Xin Qiji [7][9], and ends with a list of renowned poets and their works [4].

---

**Details**

#### I. What is Song Ci?

Song Ci is a new poetic form that emerged after Tang poetry, also known as "lyric poetry," "long-and-short-line poetry," or "poetic surplus". It was originally lyrics written to accompany music—hence the terms "filling in lyrics" (*tianci*) or "leaning on music" (*yisheng*). Ci originated in the Tang Dynasty, took shape in the Five Dynasties, and reached its peak in the Song. The complete collection *Quan Song Ci* includes about 20,000 works by over 1,300 poets [1][3].

Two major schools [4]:
- **Wanyue (Graceful and Restrained)**: gentle, sentimental, focusing on love and parting—represented by Yan Shu, Liu Yong, Li Qingzhao, Zhou Bangyan.
- **Haofang (Bold and Unrestrained)**: vigorous, heroic, breaking the tradition that ci should only deal with romance—represented by Su Shi, Xin Qiji, Lu You.

---

#### II. Cipai: The "Tune Pattern"

**Cipai** is the name of the tune pattern—like a song title today. It is not the poem's title; it simply indicates which melody the lyrics should follow. For example, *Shui Diao Ge Tou* is the cipai, while "Bright Moon, When Did You Appear?" is the actual title of Su Shi's poem.

There are over 1,000 cipai, each with a fixed format (called *cipu* or tune score). Poets must "fill in according to the tune," strictly following each cipai's rules on character count, tonal pattern, rhyme, etc. [2].

---

#### III. Three Core Rules of Ci Form (with Famous Examples)

##### Rule 1: Fixed Character and Line Count

Each cipai specifies **exactly how many characters and lines**—no adding or omitting. By length, ci are classified into [2][3]:
- **Xiaoling (Short Tune)**: ≤58 characters (e.g., *Ru Meng Ling*)
- **Zhongdiao (Medium Tune)**: 59–90 characters (e.g., *Die Lian Hua*)
- **Changdiao (Long Tune)**: ≥91 characters (e.g., *Shui Diao Ge Tou*, *Sheng Sheng Man*, *Yong Yu Le*)

**Example – Su Shi's *Shui Diao Ge Tou* (Prelude to Water Melody)** [5]

> Bright moon, when did you appear?  
> Lifting my wine, I ask the blue sky.  
> Tonight in the celestial palaces—  
> I wonder what year it is.  
> I long to ride the wind back home,  
> Yet fear the jade towers and crystal halls,  
> So high, so cold!  
> Dancing with my shadow,  
> How can this compare to the human world?  
>
> Turning the crimson pavilion,  
> Dropping through carved windows,  
> The moon shines on the sleepless.  
> Why should it bear a grudge,  
> Always turning full when we are apart?  
> People have grief and joy, partings and reunions;  
> The moon has clear and cloudy skies, waxing and waning.  
> Nothing is perfect since ancient times.  
> May we all live long,  
> And share this moonlight though a thousand miles apart.

This is a **double-tune poem of 95 characters**—a long tune. It has two sections (upper and lower): the upper section asks heaven, the lower section reminisces about loved ones [5].

---

##### Rule 2: Tonal Pattern (Level and Oblique Tones)

"Level and oblique tones" (*pingze*) refer to Chinese character tones. In ancient Chinese: **level** (*ping*) = the first tone; **oblique** (*ze*) = rising, falling, and entering tones (entering tone no longer exists in modern Mandarin). Ci has stricter tonal rules than Tang poetry—every character position has a fixed tone requirement [2].

**Example – Li Qingzhao's *Sheng Sheng Man* (Slow, Slow Tune)** [6][8]

> I search and search, I seek and seek,  
> So chill, so clear, so drear, so blank, so forlorn.  
> When suddenly warm, then suddenly cold—  
> this is the hardest time to bear.  
> Three cups of light wine, how can they fend off  
> the evening wind so sharp?  
> Wild geese pass by—they break my heart,  
> for they are my old acquaintances.
>
> Chrysanthemums are piled all over the ground,  
> withered and worn—who would pluck them now?  
> I lean by the window, how can I endure  
> this loneliness till dark?  
> The wutong trees, with drizzle dripping,  
> at dusk, drop by drop.  
> This time, how can one word "sorrow"  
> possibly contain it all?

This poem has **97 characters, with five oblique rhymes in each section**. It uses **entering-tone rhymes** throughout (all the rhymed characters are entering tone)—short, abrupt sounds that perfectly match the poem's desolate mood. The opening uses seven sets of reduplicated characters (*xunxun*, *mimi*, *lengleng*, *qingqing*, *qiqi*, *cancan*, *qiqi*), with alternating level/oblique patterns, creating a sobbing musical effect [6][8].

---

##### Rule 3: Rhyme Schemes—Flexible and Varied

Unlike Tang poetry, which only uses level-tone rhymes, ci allows level-tone rhymes, oblique-tone rhymes, or even switching between the two within one poem [2].

**Example – Xin Qiji's *Yong Yu Le* (Joy of Eternal Union) – Reminiscing at the Northern Fort of Jingkou** [7][9]

> The ancient hills and streams endure,  
> but where now is that hero Sun Zhongmou?  
> Pavilions and terraces where songs and dances once played  
> are all swept away by wind and rain.  
> The setting sun lights on withered grass and trees,  
> ordinary lanes and alleys—  
> people say the emperor Liu Yu once lived here.  
> Recall those years: with gilded spears and iron horses,  
> his might devoured ten thousand leagues like a tiger.  
>
> In the Yuanyia era, hastily sealing off the mountains,  
> he only won a panicked retreat northward.  
> Forty-three years have passed—  
> I still remember the beacon fires on the Yangzhou road.  
> How can I bear to look back?  
> Under the Buddha's temple,  
> crows and sacrificial drums fill the air.  
> Who will ask: is General Lian Po  
> still old enough to eat?

This poem has **104 characters in double-tune format, with four oblique rhymes in each section**. The oblique rhymes (all rhymed characters are oblique tone) create a sonorous, powerful rhythm that matches the poem's heroic, nostalgic tone [7][9].

---

#### IV. Structure: Single vs. Double Tune

Most ci are divided into **two sections** (upper and lower, also called *shangque* and *xiaque*). The upper section typically describes scenery or narrates events; the lower section expresses emotion or reflection. Poems without a division are called "monotone" [3].
